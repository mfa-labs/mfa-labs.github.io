Tokenization is the first step in almost every NLP pipeline, yet it is often treated as a solved problem. For high-resource languages like English or Mandarin, subword tokenization methods (BPE, WordPiece, Unigram) work reliably. But for regional languages with rich morphology and limited digital corpora, these same methods introduce structural noise that degrades downstream performance.

This post examines tokenization challenges encountered while building sentiment analysis models for **Bahasa Aceh**, a regional language of Indonesia spoken by approximately 3.5 million people.

## Why Bahasa Aceh Breaks Standard Tokenizers

### Morphological Complexity

Bahasa Aceh is agglutinative — it builds meaning through prefixes, infixes, suffixes, and circumfixes attached to root words. A single root can produce dozens of derived forms:

```
tulak (root: push)
  -> tulak (push)
  -> peutulak (cause to push)
  -> teutulak (pushed accidentally)
  -> tulakan (push repeatedly)
  -> peutulakan (cause to push repeatedly)
```

Standard BPE tokenizers trained on Indonesian or Malay data frequently split these compounds at sub-morpheme boundaries, creating fragmented representations.

### Out-of-Vocabulary Rate

Testing three common tokenizers on a 10,000-sentence Acehnese corpus:

| Tokenizer | OOV Rate | Avg. Tokens/Word |
|---|---|---|
| mBERT (WordPiece) | 38.2% | 2.8 |
| XLM-R (Unigram) | 31.7% | 2.4 |
| IndoBERT (WordPiece) | 41.5% | 3.1 |

The high OOV rate means most Acehnese words are represented as character n-grams rather than meaningful units, effectively discarding the morphological signal.

## What Worked

### Morpheme-Aware Pre-tokenization

Instead of feeding raw text to the subword tokenizer, I implemented a rule-based pre-tokenizer that identifies common Acehnese affixes:

```python
import re

ACEHNESE_PREFIXES = ['peu', 'teu', 'di', 'si', 'ka', 'meu']
ACEHNESE_SUFFIXES = ['an', 'eun', 'om', 'kah', 'lah']

def pre_tokenize_aceh(text):
    # Attach known affixes as single tokens
    for prefix in ACEHNESE_PREFIXES:
        text = re.sub(
            rf'\b({prefix})(\w+)',
            rf'{prefix}▁\2',
            text
        )
    return text
```

This simple intervention reduced the average tokens-per-word from 2.8 to 1.6 and improved sentiment classification F1 by 9.3%.

### Character-Level Fallback for OOV

For tokens that remained OOV after subword processing, a character-level CNN provided an alternative representation rather than defaulting to `[UNK]`. This was particularly effective for proper nouns and loanwords unique to the Acehnese context.

## Results

| Model | F1 (Standard) | F1 (Aceh-Adapted) |
|---|---|---|
| mBERT + fine-tune | 0.632 | 0.712 |
| IndoBERT + fine-tune | 0.598 | 0.694 |
| XLM-R + fine-tune | 0.651 | 0.738 |
| Custom CNN + affix-aware | 0.684 | 0.767 |

## Practical Recommendations

1. **Audit your tokenizer's OOV rate** before committing to a model. If it exceeds 15% for your target language, pre-tokenization adjustments will likely outperform model swapping.
2. **Morphological analysis is cheaper than more data.** A small set of language-specific rules (50-100 lines of regex) produced larger gains than adding 5,000 additional training samples.
3. **Character-level fallbacks are underused.** Most transformer implementations default to `[UNK]` when they could fall through to a character representation with minimal architecture changes.

## Future Work

I am exploring byte-level tokenization (ByT5-style) as a language-agnostic alternative that sidesteps the pre-tokenization problem entirely. Early results show competitive performance on Acehnese, though at significantly higher computational cost.
